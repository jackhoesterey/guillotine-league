# The Guillotine Playbook — 2026

**Your league:** 18 teams · Full PPR · Initial draft, then $1,000 FAAB · One team chopped every week

**Written for:** someone who has never played a guillotine league and doesn't follow football closely. Everything you need is in here. No prior knowledge assumed.

---

## Part 1 — What you're actually playing

### The one-sentence version

Every week, all 18 teams score points. The team that scores the **fewest** points is eliminated, permanently, and all 14 of their players are dumped onto the free-agent pool for everyone else to bid on. Last team alive wins.

### What's different about it

In a normal fantasy league you play one opponent per week and you're trying to win. Half the league wins every week. You can start 0-4 and still make the playoffs.

In a guillotine league you play *all seventeen* opponents at once, and you're not trying to win — **you're trying not to finish last.** There is no recovery from a bad week. There is no playoff seeding. There is only alive or dead.

This changes what a "good player" means. In a normal league you want the guy who scores 4 points one week and 32 the next, because that 32 wins you a matchup. In guillotine, that 4-point week is the week you die. **You want boring. You want the same 12–15 points every single Sunday.** Floor beats ceiling, and it isn't close.

### The 2026 calendar

The NFL regular season runs **Wednesday, September 9, 2026 through January 10, 2027** — 18 weeks, each team playing 17 games with one bye.

With 18 teams and one chop per week, your league is decided after **Week 17** (Sunday, January 3, 2027). Week 18 doesn't matter, which is convenient — Week 18 is when good teams rest their starters and fantasy scores go haywire.

Here's the whole season at a glance:

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 18 | 0 | — |
| 2 | 17 | 14 | — |

Read that third column again. By Week 8 there are ninety-eight formerly-drafted players sitting in free agency. By Week 12 there are more free agents than there are rostered players in the entire league.

**This single fact drives almost every decision you make.**

## Part 2 — The three ideas that win guillotine leagues

### Idea 1: Your roster is temporary. Your FAAB is the real asset.

You will not win this league with the team you draft. Nobody does. The winner's Week 17 roster typically has 3–5 players from their original draft and the rest bought off the scrap heap.

So the draft is not about building a champion. **The draft is about building something that survives to Week 5**, at which point the free-agent pool becomes rich enough that you can rebuild in-flight.

Your $1,000 FAAB is the thing that actually converts into a championship. Treat it like capital, not like pocket money.

### Idea 2: The bar rises every single week.

Week 2, you're competing against 17 rosters, several of which are bad. Week 12, you're competing against 6 rosters, all of which have been buying the best available player for three months.

A team that scores a flat 115 points every week is comfortably safe in Week 2 and comfortably dead by Week 12 — without doing anything wrong.

**You cannot stand still.** Every week the survivors get stronger, which means the elimination line climbs. Rough shape of it in an 18-team full-PPR league:

| Stage | Typical chop line | Your target |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–4 | ~80–90 pts | Don't have a disaster |
| Weeks 5–9 | ~90–100 pts | Be clearly mid-pack |
| Weeks 10–13 | ~100–110 pts | Be top half every week |
| Weeks 14–17 | ~105–120 pts | Be genuinely good |

(Treat these as rough. The tracker spreadsheet measures your league's *actual* line each week, which is far more useful than my estimate.)

The people who lose this league are usually the ones who hoard FAAB, look safe in October, and get chopped in Week 11 sitting on $800 they never spent. **Unspent FAAB at the moment you're eliminated is the most expensive thing in fantasy football.**

### Idea 3: Most eliminations are self-inflicted.

Go look at any guillotine league's elimination log. The chopped team usually didn't get outplayed. They started a player who was declared inactive 90 minutes before kickoff. They forgot a bye week. They left an injured guy in the lineup. They never logged in.

In an 18-team league, **one zero in your starting lineup is roughly a 12-point swing, and 12 points is usually the difference between 14th and last.**

The single highest-return habit in this entire document: **check your lineup at 11:00 AM Eastern every Sunday.** Inactive lists drop 90 minutes before each game. That one alarm on your phone will outperform every clever bidding strategy in Part 4.

## Part 3 — The draft

### Format math for an 18-team league

Assume a fairly standard guillotine setup — confirm these with your commissioner before draft day:

- **Starting lineup:** 1 QB, 2 RB, 2 WR, 1 TE, 1 FLEX (RB/WR/TE) — roughly 9 starters
- **Roster:** 14 players (so ~5 bench spots)
- **No kickers, no defenses** (most guillotine leagues drop them — they're pure noise, and noise is what kills you)
- **18 rounds × 18 teams = 252 total picks**

That last number matters. 252 picks means the draft goes *deep* — deeper than any normal league. Players you've never heard of will be drafted. Every starting running back in the NFL will be gone. You need a board that runs 250 deep, which is what the cheat sheet is for.

### What supply looks like at each position

| Position | Starters the league needs | Genuinely reliable options in the NFL | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| QB | 18 | ~24 | Comfortable |
| TE | 18 | ~14 | **Tight** |
| RB | 36 (2 per team) + flex | ~28 true workhorses | **The scarcest thing on the board** |
| WR | 36 + flex | ~55 | Deep |

**Running back is the scarce resource.** After roughly RB28 you are into committee backfields and guessing. In an 18-team league those go fast.

But — and this is the guillotine wrinkle — running backs also get injured more than anyone else, and injuries are what kill you here. So the answer isn't "draft five running backs." The answer is:

### The draft plan

**Rounds 1–4 — buy volume, not upside.**
Take the players who touch the ball the most, on offenses that score. A running back who gets 18 carries plus 4 catches every week is worth more to you than a receiver who might go for 150 yards and two touchdowns. Don't get cute. Don't reach for a rookie.

**Rounds 5–8 — fill your starting lineup with floor.**
Target receivers who get 7+ targets a week even when they're not the story. Take your tight end here if a reliable one is sitting there — the TE cliff in an 18-team league is brutal and a zero at TE is a real elimination risk.

**Rounds 9–13 — take a quarterback, and take bodies who can start.**
Don't chase a top-3 quarterback early. In a 1-QB league with 32 starting NFL quarterbacks, there is always a usable one available, and a running quarterback in the middle rounds gives you nearly the same weekly floor for a fraction of the cost. Spend the early picks on positions where supply actually runs out.

**Rounds 14–18 — handcuffs and warm bodies, not lottery tickets.**
In a normal league you swing for upside here. In guillotine, you want players who would score 8–10 points if forced into your lineup tomorrow. Specifically: **handcuff your own running backs** (draft the backup to your own starters). If your RB1 goes down in Week 3, his backup instantly becomes a top-20 back, and you got him for free instead of paying $200 for him on waivers.

### Hard rules for draft day

**Do:**
- Draft the boring guy. Every time. Volume, target share, three-down role.
- Take the player on the good offense over the more talented player on a bad one.
- Handcuff your own starting running backs late.
- Have a backup plan at every position for Weeks 6–8 (see byes below).

**Don't:**
- **Don't stack.** Never take three players from the same NFL team. When that offense has a bad Sunday, you don't have a bad Sunday — you have an elimination.
- **Don't draft injury reclamation projects.** A player coming off an ACL tear, a 30-year-old running back, a guy with five soft-tissue injuries in three years. In a normal league you take the discount. Here you take the exit.
- **Don't draft rookies expecting them to figure it out by November.** You may not be alive in November.
- **Don't draft two quarterbacks or two tight ends early.** Bench spots are for handcuffs and floor. There will be 70 free agents by Week 6.
- **Don't overthink bye weeks in the draft** — see the next section.

### Bye weeks: a chore, not a strategy

Everyone panics about bye weeks. In this format they barely matter, and here's the proof: by Week 8, when four teams are on bye, there are **98 chopped players sitting in free agency**. You will always be able to field a lineup.

Two genuine bye-week notes for 2026:

1. **No team is on bye until Week 5** (KC and CAR). Getting to Week 5 is the only real early goal.
2. **Week 11 is the monster** — six teams on bye (ATL, CLE, GB, LAR, NE, SEA). If you're rostering Puka Nacua, A.J. Brown, Drake London, Davante Adams, Josh Jacobs, Kyren Williams or Drake Maye, you'll be patching multiple holes that week. Fine — there will be 140 free agents. Just don't be asleep.

And here's the counterintuitive edge nobody talks about: **heavy bye weeks are chaos weeks.** When six teams are out, everybody's scores compress and the results get random. Random is dangerous — good teams get chopped in chaos weeks. So Weeks 6, 7, 8 and especially 11 are exactly the weeks where paying up for a clean, bye-free starting lineup buys you the most survival probability per dollar. Most managers do the opposite and try to save money through the byes.

## Part 4 — FAAB: the actual game

You get **$1,000**, and it has to last up to 17 weeks. This is where leagues are won and lost.

### The core principle

Money is only worth what it buys you. **The value of a dollar goes up over the season** (players get much cheaper as more teams are eliminated and fewer bidders remain), **but the value of surviving is infinite** (you can't spend anything if you're dead).

So the rule is not "save money" and it's not "spend money." It is:

> **Spend the minimum required to make this week safe, and not one dollar more — unless the player available will make every remaining week safe.**

### What the market actually looks like

Real guillotine league data on winning bids for the same elite player at different points in the season:

| Month | Typical winning bid for a top-15 player |
|---|---|
| September | ~$485 |
| October | ~$310 |
| November | ~$220 |
| December | ~$50 |

That's the whole argument for patience in one table. The same player costs ten times more in September than in December, because in September you're bidding against 16 rivals with full wallets and in December you're bidding against four broke ones.

### The spend curve

Aim to have roughly this much of your $1,000 remaining at the end of each stretch:

| Stage | Weeks | Budget remaining target | Mindset |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Survive** | 1–4 | **$850+** | Cheap patches only. You're ~90% to survive these weeks without doing anything. |
| **Navigate** | 5–9 | **$650+** | Bye patching and streamers. Spend on the chaos weeks (6, 7, 8), not between them. |
| **Pivot** | 10–13 | **$250–350** | The bar has risen and prices have collapsed. This is when you buy the roster that wins. |
| **Empty the tank** | 14–17 | **→ $0** | Every dollar you carry into your final week is a dollar you wasted. |

If you make it to Week 14 with money left, you have made a mistake — unless you're using it that week.

### How much to bid on a given player

Set your ceiling by what the player *is*, then adjust for how much danger you're in:

| Player type | Weeks 1–4 | Weeks 5–9 | Weeks 10–13 | Weeks 14–17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Elite** (top-15 overall, wins you weeks for the rest of the season) | up to $250 | up to $200 | up to $175 | up to everything |
| **Real starter** (would start on your team all year) | up to $75 | up to $125 | up to $150 | up to everything |
| **Bye/injury patch** (starts for you 2–4 weeks) | up to $25 | up to $40 | up to $60 | whatever it takes |
| **Streamer** (one good matchup) | up to $8 | up to $15 | up to $25 | whatever it takes |
| **Handcuff / lottery ticket** | $1–3 | $1–5 | $1–10 | $1–15 |

**Then apply the danger multiplier.** Before bids lock, ask yourself honestly: *if this week went normally, would I be in the bottom three?*

- **Comfortably mid-pack →** bid at the low end of the range, or don't bid at all.
- **Bottom five →** bid at the top of the range.
- **Genuinely likely to be chopped →** the ranges don't apply. Bid whatever it takes. A 40% chance of surviving with $100 left beats a 15% chance of surviving with $600 left.

### Six bidding tactics that are basically free money

1. **Bid odd numbers.** Bid $77, not $75. Bid $153, not $150. Ties are broken by some arbitrary rule you don't control, and an enormous number of blind-bid auctions are decided by round-number collisions. This costs you two dollars and wins you players.

2. **Bid what he's worth to you, not what you think others will bid.** Blind bidding is not poker. You cannot outmaneuver anyone. Trying to "win by a dollar" just means losing by a dollar.

3. **Read the transaction log every week.** Most platforms publish winning bid amounts. Keep a running count of who has how much left. If four of the eight surviving teams are under $100, your $120 bid wins everything. This is the single biggest information edge in the format and almost nobody does it.

4. **Spread $1 bids everywhere.** On weeks where you have nothing to chase, throw $1–2 at four or five decent bench players. Half of them will get through unopposed. Free depth costs you nothing and prevents emergencies later.

5. **Never spend more than ~35% of your remaining budget on one player before Week 10.** The exception is a genuine top-15 overall player in the first three weeks — that's the one time overpaying early is right, because you're buying 15 weeks of raised floor, not a patch.

6. **Don't bid on a player who doesn't crack your starting lineup.** In a 14-man roster with 9 starters, a bench player has to be either a handcuff to someone you own or a bye-week fill. "Best player available" is a redraft habit. Kill it.

### The Week 1 trap

After Week 1, the first chopped roster hits waivers — and it will contain at least one first- or second-round pick, possibly someone like a consensus top-10 overall player. It will feel like the opportunity of the season.

Half your league will spend $300–500 chasing him. Some will spend more.

**They are usually wrong.** Not because he isn't good, but because they've spent a third of their capital in the week they were 94% likely to survive anyway, and they will be broke and helpless in November when the pool is full of the same quality at a tenth of the price.

The exception, and it's real: if a genuine top-8 overall player is on that list, $200–250 is defensible. He's not a patch, he's a floor-raiser for 16 straight weeks and he removes an entire category of risk from your season. Just don't do it twice, and don't go past $250 in September.

## Part 5 — Your weekly routine

Set these as recurring phone reminders. Seriously. The routine is worth more than the strategy.

| When | What you do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Tuesday evening** | Chopped roster hits free agency. Review all 14 players. Decide who you actually want. | You have all week; don't decide in a panic. |
| **Wednesday (before your league's deadline)** | Submit FAAB bids. Odd numbers. Check the budget curve first. | Waivers typically process Wednesday morning. |
| **Wednesday morning** | Read the transaction log. **Write down what everyone spent.** | This is your information edge. Takes 3 minutes. |
| **Thursday ~7:30pm ET** | Lock in any Thursday-night players. | A late scratch on TNF is unrecoverable. |
| **Saturday** | Set your full lineup. Check the injury report for every starter. | Do the thinking when you're not rushed. |
| **🔴 SUNDAY 11:00 AM ET** | **FINAL LINEUP CHECK.** Inactives are announced 90 minutes before kickoff. Bench anyone listed out or doubtful. | **This is the most important item in this entire document.** |
| **Sunday 4:00 PM ET** | Quick check for late-window scratches. | Second wave of inactives. |
| **Monday night** | Note your score, the chop line, and who got eliminated. Log it in the tracker. | The tracker learns your league's real elimination threshold. |

## Part 6 — The ten mistakes that get people chopped

1. **Not checking the lineup on Sunday morning.** One inactive player, one elimination. This is the number one cause of death.
2. **Hoarding FAAB into November.** Getting chopped with $700 in the bank is the most common way a *good* team dies.
3. **Blowing the budget in Week 1** on a shiny name during the week they were safe anyway.
4. **Drafting for upside.** Boom-bust players are elimination machines. The 4-point week always comes.
5. **Stacking one NFL team.** One bad Sunday for that offense, one bad Sunday for you.
6. **Not tracking opponents' remaining budget.** You're bidding blind against people you could be reading.
7. **Playing to win the week.** You don't get anything for first place in Week 6. Optimize for a high floor, not a high score.
8. **Panic-drafting bye week coverage.** By Week 8 there are 98 free agents. Relax.
9. **Not rostering your own handcuffs.** When your RB1 tears something, his backup will cost $200 on waivers — or $0 in Round 16.
10. **Going quiet in October.** The chop line climbs ~25 points over the season. Standing still is moving backwards.

---

## Part 7 — The one-page summary

If you remember nothing else:

- **You're trying not to finish last, not trying to finish first.** Floor over ceiling, always.
- **The draft only has to get you to Week 5.** After that the free-agent pool rebuilds your team for you.
- **Your $1,000 is the real roster.** Protect it early, deploy it hard from Week 10, finish at zero.
- **Check your lineup at 11:00 AM Eastern every Sunday.** More eliminations come from inactive players than from bad drafting.
- **Read the transaction log.** Knowing who's broke is worth more than knowing who's good.
- **The bar rises every week.** A team that doesn't improve gets chopped around Week 11 without ever playing badly.

Good luck. The format is brutal and completely addictive.
